How to use this

Three parts, two purposes. The glossary and the insights & practices library are card sets you filter and search when you need something — before a session, or when a client says a word you want to be sure about. This middle part is the bit you read once and then argue with.

Cards come in two kinds. An insight is something to understand — it changes how you read what a client is telling you. A practice is something to do or say. Where there's a useful line, it's on the card in quotes; steal it or reword it.

The research sits in a footnote behind each card, with a strength label. That's deliberate — you almost never need the numbers with a client, you need to know how much weight the thing will bear. Open the footnote when you want to check, or when a client asks how you know.

One organising principle runs through all of it. The concepts are well-evidenced; the coaching applications are reasoned inferences from them. The most-cited review in the field says as much explicitly. Cards whose footnote reads Inference & guidance are exactly that — worth doing, cheap to try, and yours to own rather than the literature's.

What the paradigm actually claims

Neurodiversity is a fact about a population, like biodiversity. A group can be neurodiverse; a person is neurodivergent. The paradigm is a philosophical position — that this variation is natural and valuable, and that there is no one normal brain. The movement is social justice activism. They are three different things, and a client can be neurodivergent while holding none of these positions.

That last point matters more than it sounds. Many clients want practical accommodations and hold a straightforwardly medical view of their own condition. Some experience their neurotype primarily as a disability and find the celebratory framing erasing. Don't assume alignment, and don't perform the politics at someone who came for help with a promotion.

The correction coaches most need

The paradigm does not claim that neurodivergent conditions cause no difficulty, that support is unnecessary, or that all difficulty is socially produced. The mainstream position is social-relational: disability arises from the interaction between a person and an environment built for a different profile. That leaves ample room for intrinsic difficulty. Over-correcting into “it's all just difference” is a way of not listening.

The three models are worth being able to distinguish cleanly, because you'll move between them in a single session. The medical model locates difficulty in the person and intervenes there. The social model separates impairment from disability and intervenes in the environment. The biopsychosocial model treats biological, psychological and social factors as interacting, and licenses working at all three levels at once. Doyle's 2020 review — the standard workplace reference — explicitly advances the third, and is routinely miscited as advancing the second.

Where coaching actually sits

You don't diagnose, and you don't need to. Self-identification is sufficient for coaching purposes, and much of the adult population that fits these patterns is undiagnosed — particularly women, people of colour, and anyone who succeeded academically. Take self-identification as client-held self-knowledge, without confirming, disconfirming, or implying a diagnosis, and be able to say clearly what coaching can and cannot establish.

If a client hasn't named it, coach the presenting need. Speculating about someone's neurotype is not within scope, however well-intentioned, and however obvious it seems to you.

The honest position on your own evidence base: coaching is among the most widely practised and least evidenced interventions in this field. Two of 143 studies in the field's own systematic review evaluated it at all. What you can defensibly do is borrow the components that are evidenced — skills training, structure, and the working alliance — rather than claim an evidence base coaching doesn't have. That's not a weakness to hide. It's a reason to stay curious in the room rather than confident.

Nobody is going to give you a framework

There is no competency framework, position statement, or recognised credential for coaching neurodivergent clients from the ICF, EMCC, or the Association for Coaching. What exists is CPD webinars. There is no published guidance on what to do when a client self-identifies without a diagnosis, on distinguishing autistic burnout from depression, or on trauma surfacing after a late identification. The ICF's own referral guidance — the most concrete document in the field — mentions neither ADHD, autism, nor neurodiversity.

PAAC is the only body with an ADHD-specific credential, and it is self-governing, not recognised by any coaching body or regulator. Anyone advertising a “certified neurodiversity coach” credential is describing a private course completion certificate.

What affirming practice rules out

The concept is most developed in occupational therapy and speech and language therapy, where a 2024 scoping review found eight sources and explicitly noted the absence of definitional consensus. No coaching body has adopted any of it. Importing it is a reasoned borrowing from adjacent professions — worth doing, worth labelling as such.

Translated into coaching, affirming practice rules out:

  • Any goal whose success criterion is appearing more neurotypical — more eye contact, less stimming, “professional” affect, masking adopted as an unexamined career strategy.
  • Framing accommodation needs as excuses or mindset problems.
  • Fixing executive function rather than building environmental scaffolding around it.
  • Coach-set rather than client-set goals.

If masking is the strategy — and sometimes it legitimately is — make it a conscious, time-boxed choice the client owns, with the costs named, rather than a silent default.

The counterweight: affirming is not uncritical. Telling a client their difficulty isn't real, or that the right environment will dissolve every friction, isn't affirming. And not every neurodivergent client wants a redesigned session. Ask.

What changes in the room

Little of this is trial-tested. It follows from the mechanisms in the evidence library and from practitioner consensus, and it's cheap enough that the asymmetry favours doing it.

  • Ask, don't infer. “What helps you think best in a conversation like this?” and “How do you prefer to receive things between sessions?” Ninety seconds, better data than any assumption from a category.
  • Make the implicit explicit. Agendas up front, time signposts, an explicit close. Ambiguity you experience as spaciousness may land as an unanswerable demand.
  • Send things in writing. Summary, agreed next step, dates. Verbal-only agreements ask the client to do retrieval work you could have done for them.
  • Allow processing time. Silence after a good question is often the work happening. Sending a key question ahead of a session is a legitimate adaptation, not a spoiler.
  • Contract for the missed step before it's missed. For a client with a long history of falling short, “keep me accountable” can quietly recreate the mechanism that has hurt them repeatedly.
  • Smaller steps than feel necessary, external scaffolding, and a named next action with a time attached. Do it live where you can.

Language

Default to identity-first — autistic, dyslexic, dyspraxic — ask each client, mirror what they use, and be explicit that yours is a default rather than a correction of theirs. Two things are worth knowing beyond that rule.

ADHD has no community consensus. In the largest recent UK survey, “person with ADHD” and “ADHDer” rated similarly. This is a genuine disagreement, not a settled convention you can look up.

“Difference, not disorder” is contested by neurodivergent people themselves. “Disorder” is overwhelmingly disliked — but “difference” is read by a meaningful group as watering down real disability and undermining claims to accommodation and legal protection. That bites hardest in exactly the conversation where you'd reach for it. Don't perform the reframe; ask what the client wants their difficulty called.

Other substitutions worth having automatic: special interests → focused or passionate interests; symptoms → traits or characteristics; comorbid → co-occurring; suffers from → is; high/low functioning → describe the actual strengths and support needs. Functioning labels are worth dropping on empirical grounds, not just courtesy — IQ explains almost none of the variance in adaptive functioning, so “high functioning” describes one thing while implying another.

Six ways this goes wrong

  • Category over person. Two ADHD clients can need opposite things. The label narrows the hypothesis space; it doesn't answer the question.
  • Strengths-washing. Superpower framing feels affirming and reads as a refusal to acknowledge real difficulty. Variance, not elevation, is both more honest and more useful.
  • Coaching the mask. Helping a client perform neurotypicality more convincingly may raise short-term outcomes and raises the documented costs alongside them.
  • Drifting clinical. Burnout, trauma and years of accumulated shame surface fast in this work. Notice, name, refer.
  • Importing internet constructs uncritically. RSD is the current example. Validate the experience; stay agnostic on the taxonomy.
  • Over-adapting. Not every neurodivergent client wants a redesigned session. Ask first.

Figures that circulate and shouldn't

Four numbers you will meet constantly, each of which needs a caveat attached or dropping entirely.

  • “15–20% of people are neurodivergent.” Real, correctly attributed to Doyle (2020), and much softer than its ubiquity suggests. It is one author's reasoned synthesis of four prevalence figures adjusted for overlap — she calls it “a reasonable estimate” — and it partly rests on the untraceable dyslexia figure below. Because “neurodivergent” has no diagnostic definition, no precise number is possible in principle. Quote it as an order-of-magnitude indicator, never as a statistic.
  • “10% of people are dyslexic.” Dyslexia Action published a statement in 2023 saying the original source of this figure is untraceable and that organisations including themselves had been repeating an unsourced statistic. Use “5–20% depending where the threshold sits”.
  • “14% of adults have ADHD.” A screening-instrument positive rate from APMS 2023/4, being widely reported as a prevalence rate. It is not one. Use 2.5–3.5%.
  • “0.01% of adults have Tourette's.” An ascertainment artefact — adults are rarely assessed. The same review reports that 50–80% of people over 16 still experience at least mild tics.

Two more worth carrying. “Bradshaw et al. on ADHD masking” does not appear to exist — it circulates in practitioner discussion, doesn't survive a search, and isn't cited by the field's own 2026 review. It looks like a conflation with the autism camouflaging literature. And there is no validated ADHD-specific camouflaging measure; the commercial instruments on clinic sites describe themselves as unvalidated. Don't administer one.
